## Anchoring

Anchor high before you show the real price. The first number reframes every
number after it.

- Three tiers: high anchor on the right, the tier you want sold in the middle,
  a low option that exists to justify the middle and feed later upsells.
- The anchor does not have to be your product — **it only needs to be in
  frame**. Luxury stores stock the $900 candle so the $200 candle reads as
  affordable, not to sell the $900 candle.
- No premium SKU yet? Put the competing number in frame yourself — list the
  typical retail price next to yours (the Keen move):

```
Typical retail: $120    Our price: $75   ← the anchor is in frame; it isn't yours
```

- Retail runs good / better / best on every shelf. Best sells rarely but
  anchors; the middle carries the volume; good catches the price-only buyer.

## Price is a quality signal

Buyers cannot verify quality before purchase — "100% cashmere" spans garbage to
heirloom under the same label — so they read price as the proxy.
**Underpricing is a brand problem, not a margin problem.**

- Streetwear: boutique buyers treat $400–500 as the signal that a shirt is
  designer-tier. A better-made $80 shirt gets read as "must be lesser" — the
  low price lowers the expectation, and you never get the chance to exceed it.
- Price inside the band your positioning claims. A premium claim with a value
  price is a contradiction the buyer resolves against you.

## Charm vs rounded pricing

$9.99 vs $10 is one cent of price and a category of psychology.

| Ending | Use in | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 9-ending (.99, 49, 99) | Mass, value, deal-driven categories | 9-endings consistently drive higher volume |
| Rounded (10, 100, 500) | Premium and luxury | The round number says "we know you're smarter than the trick" |

**Lost price points:** buyers cluster at 49 / 59 / 99. $69 and $79 are dead
zones — 79 reads as 99 anyway (take the 99), 69 reads as far more than 59
(drop to 59 or 49).

## The product-mix ladder

Four roles. Every SKU decision maps to exactly one.

| Role | Job | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Entry** | Low-risk, high-trial door into the ecosystem — must be a banger, it sets the expectation for everything above it | Aesop $27 hand soap · Apple iPhone SE / AirPods · Loro Piana $400–600 scarves, beanies, small leather · Glossier $18 brow |
| **Core** | The real business — the repeat purchase most people actually buy | Aesop $60–100 skincare · iPhone / MacBook Air · Loro Piana $1,500–3,000 knitwear and outerwear · Glossier $35 skincare |
| **Aspirational** | Sells little, anchors every price below it, positions the brand a tier up | Aesop $200 kits and fragrance · MacBook Pro / Pro Display · Loro Piana $12,000 Gift of Kings jacket · Glossier $75 fragrance · the $2,000 jacket on a denim-and-tees brand page |
| **Halo** | May not even be for sale — defines the ceiling of what the brand could be | Palace's ~$40k car listed in a shoot · District Vision's $10,000 one-of-one bike at its cycling launch · Vision Pro ("makes a $1,600 phone feel reasonable") |

**The Oreo lesson:** the collab and vanity SKUs (Swedish Fish Oreos, Lady Gaga
Oreos) sell a little themselves — and drive far more sales of the main line.
They pull people to the shelf and give them something to talk about. Judge the
top of the ladder by what it does to the core, not by its own revenue.

**The two classic mistakes:**

1. **Too many, too quick.** Build the core, get real traction, hit walls —
   then expand the ladder.
2. **Only the middle.** No entry point to acquire cheaply (and "core on sale"
   as the entry is the same mistake wearing a discount), and no aspirational
   tier — nothing to anchor against, nothing bigger to believe in.

Services run the identical ladder:

```
$99 audit        → entry (acquires + qualifies)
$1,000/mo retainer → core (the business)
```

## Personas from real data

Whiteboard personas are fiction. Paid surveys sample the one customer type that
answers surveys. Build personas from the purchase data itself.

- Run the customer base / email list through an enrichment tool (Outer Signal
  class). Real output looks like: 14% high-performance executives, 10.8%
  affluent health investors, plus wellness professionals and athlete
  biohackers — each segment with its own LTV and repeat rate.
- Same run finds influencers and UGC creators already buying from you
  (133 orders → 7 ultra-high-net-worth, 6 influencers, 3 industry experts).
- Segment email/SMS by persona **and LTV**, then act on the gap:
  one-and-done segment → flows engineered to win the second purchase;
  high-LTV but under-mailed segment → raise touch frequency and test the ceiling.
- **One landing page per persona.** The wellness professional buying for the
  job and the biohacker buying for himself need different headlines, bullets,
  proof, and FAQ. Duplicate the lander, swap the copy and images. The deepest
  rift is always *buying for work* vs *buying for me* — split that first.
- Load persona data + brand materials into a Claude project and generate:

```
1) landing / email / SMS / ad copy per persona
2) product messaging tuned to the actual base
3) the segmentation call itself — 5 personas rarely need 5 messages;
   ask which personas one message serves, and split only where messages diverge
```

## Hormozi: the 9-step skeleton

```
1 who you are → 2 who to sell to → 3 what to sell → 4 get them to buy
→ 5 get them to buy more times → 6 who helps you (team)
→ 7 keep your advantage → 8 stick with it → 9 never stop getting better
```

Steps 4–5 mechanics (CLOSER, the eight money levers) live in `11`. This file
takes 1–3.

### Traits are bundled terms — install behaviors, not adjectives

"Charismatic" decompresses to: nod while they talk, repeat back what they said,
smile, speak with volume, vary tone and pace, emphasize key words, firm
handshake, eye contact. Do the behaviors and the label follows. Same for
skills: "build a company" → advertise + sell + build product + hire — each
decomposable further, down to "turn on the computer." Whenever someone is
stuck, break the bundle down one level.

**Do → be → have.** The doing creates the having, and the being is what others
call you after enough doing. Doing is the only lever you control — start there.

### What to sell: pain / profession / passion

| P | Source | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Pain** | A problem you overcame | Allergy-safe school lunches you cracked · the depression you climbed out of · the Google PPC you taught yourself |
| **Profession** | Fractionalize your job skill across many companies | Whatever one employer pays you for, several will rent |
| **Passion** | An interest with buyers attached | Model cars → kits, builds, content, events |

Default buyer: **someone like you.** "Living with the problem" is a literal
YC/a16z investment criterion — years of exposure to the problem and the failed
solutions. You skip market research because you already know the vocabulary
and the innate desires: they're yours.

### The 4 market criteria

Pick the market before the product. Score all four or walk:

1. **Money** — can they pay? Everyone broke = find another market, however
   good the offer.
2. **Easy to find** — directories, associations, conferences you can show up to?
3. **Growing** — tailwind, not headwind. US newspapers shrank 25%/yr
   compounding; the vendor selling to them had to win 25% market share every
   year just to stay flat — and the survivors had no money anyway.
4. **In pain** — searing when possible, but even the chore level counts:
   things people would rather not learn and rather not do.

(These four overlay the 6-criteria niche score in `02` — run both.)

### Who you sell to is the price

- "It's easier to sell 100 people a $10,000 thing than 10,000 people a $100
  thing." The hidden cost of a low price is the distribution it demands.
- The old-car story: pawn shop offers $2,500 → dealership $5,000 → collectors'
  auction house $100,000. **Same car. Who you sell to changes everything.**
  Before concluding "I suck at business," check who you're selling to.
- The CRO consultant: +10% on a $1M store adds $100k; +10% on a $100M store
  adds $10M. Same work, 100× the value created, priced accordingly. The
  biggest lever on success: the same work for better clients.
- Sell the concepts, not the deliverable — the agency that switched from
  selling design hours to selling ideas moved from fees to millions; execution
  commoditizes, the idea doesn't.

### The six things anyone can sell

| Type | What it really is |
|---|---|
| Products | Physical, tangible goods |
| Services | Time — time-to-learn + time-to-do (an information advantage plus execution) |
| Software / SaaS | The above, digitized |
| Access | Physical (real estate, events) or digital (community, media, education) |
| Risk | Insurance |
| Money | Banking, lending |

One passion, six businesses: sell the model cars (product), build them for
people (service), a design app (SaaS), meets and races (access), insure
collections (risk), finance purchases (money). Pick the vehicle that matches
the market's money and pain.
